XP PLANNING PACKET

We do not need a grand system. We need a plan that a tired student can read at midnight and still use.

This packet is the underground answer to the question: “How do we teach students how to plan without turning them into consultants?”

1. The rule

  1. Pick one tiny value.
  2. Write it as a card.
  3. Make the card testable.
  4. Put the card on the board.
  5. Do the smallest slice that proves the value.
  6. Show the result, then change the plan.

2. Invariant tokens

  • 📌 One card, one value, one job.
  • 🧪 Done means proof, not vibes.
  • ⏱️ Time box it. Stop at the wall.
  • 🔁 Inspect, adapt, and replan.
  • 🧠 No big design up front. Sketch enough.
  • 🪜 Split the work into visible steps.

3. The lesson plan for students

  1. Warm-up: Ask, “What is the smallest useful thing?”
  2. Card writing: Each student writes three cards in plain language.
  3. Planning game: Sort cards by value and risk. Keep the board honest.
  4. Estimate: Use sizes like S / M / L or 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 ideal hours.
  5. Proof line: Every card gets one sentence that says how we will know it is done.
  6. Show and adapt: Build one slice, demo it, then update the plan.

This teaches planning by making students plan a real slice, then change the plan after they see the work.

4. What we leave behind

  • A story card for each small promise.
  • A proof line for each card.
  • A short risk note.
  • A tiny release plan for the next visible demo.
  • A one-line retro note for what changed.

5. GameFAQs-style help

  • Q: The request is vague. A: Write the smallest useful card and ask for proof.
  • Q: The plan is wrong. A: Good. Change it before it becomes a ritual.
  • Q: How many cards do we plan? A: Enough for one slice, not the whole empire.
  • Q: What if the task is too big? A: Break it until the card is small enough to touch.

6. Found artifacts

  • Field note: “Do not write essays. Write cards.”
  • Sample card from the old kiosk
  • Incident report: the fake 404 trap and what the class missed

7. The lesson

Students learn planning by doing it in public, with blunt limits and visible evidence. The plan is not sacred. It is a tool that gets corrected by reality.
